How alike are the four channel coefficients

A two by two channel is four numbers: h11, h12, h21 and h22. How different those four numbers are from one another is decided by the room, not by the radio. Start with a room that has nothing to scatter off and add scatterers one at a time.

An empty room offers the signal one direction to travel in, the direct path. Every scatterer opens one more. The panel on the right draws the four coefficients as four arrows: the length of an arrow is the gain of that path and its angle is the phase. Watch how alike the four arrows are, and how that changes as the room fills up.

Two kinds of sameness, and they break at different speeds

With one direction the four coefficients are about as alike as four numbers can be, and in two separate ways. Every arrow is exactly the same length, because a single path reaches all four antenna pairs with the same gain; the tiny antenna spacing changes when a copy arrives but not how strong it is. And the four angles are locked together: the angle of h22 is always the angle of h12 plus the angle of h21 minus the angle of h11. Four complex numbers are on the screen, but only two of them are being freely chosen.

The two break apart on different timetables, which is the part worth showing the class. One scatterer destroys the equal lengths immediately, because the second path reaches the four antenna pairs with four different combined gains. The phase lock is much more stubborn: with a single scatterer the relation is still nearly intact in a large share of rooms, and it takes several scatterers before it reliably breaks.

Alike means the longest gain is under 1.3 times the shortest; nearly locked means the phase relation holds to within 10 degrees. The slide quotes slightly different figures because it places the scattering directions a different way; the pattern, and the gap between the two columns, is the same.

Things worth trying

Set the room to 0 and press rearrange as many times as you like. The longest divided by the shortest stays at exactly 1.00 and the phase relation stays at exactly 0 degrees. That is not luck. One direction makes H a single column multiplied by a single row, and such a matrix cannot have four independent entries however the room is arranged.

Set it to 1 and rearrange a few times. The lengths separate at once, but keep an eye on the phase figure: it often stays small. Then set it to 8 and rearrange: both numbers now land somewhere unhelpful only rarely.

Notice what does not change anything: the number of antennas. More antennas in a room with one direction produce more coefficients, all of them still the same length and still locked to each other. The variety has to come from the room.
