One carrier or four, through the same room

The same sixteen bits are sent twice through the same room: once as one fast stream on a single carrier, and once split across four slower subcarriers. Nothing about the room changes between the two runs. Step through and watch where the two chains part company, which is not at the transmitter and not in the air, but at the moment the receiver has to decide what each symbol was.

What to look at

  • The room is one echo, and it is the same room in both runs. A direct copy, and a second copy 0.9 as strong arriving 50 ns later, which is 15 m of extra path. That is the echo amplitude every other page in this course uses, and 15 m is an ordinary large room. Nothing else is in the model: no noise, no movement, no second room.
  • The single carrier runs at 20 Msym/s, so a symbol lasts 50 ns. The echo is therefore exactly one symbol late, and every symbol arrives on top of the one before it. That is inter-symbol interference, and panel 2 shows it as a time picture rather than as a phrase. It is also the same statement as the curve in panel 4: a channel that adds 0.9 of the previous sample in time is a channel whose response is 1 + 0.9 e−j2πfT in frequency. One room, two pictures. The pink block in panel 4 is this single carrier lying across the whole of it.
  • One block is eight symbols on eight subcarriers, and that is the point. A block carries exactly one constellation symbol per subcarrier, so 32 bits of QPSK make 16 symbols and need two blocks of eight. Each subcarrier symbol lasts 400 ns instead of 50 ns, because eight of them are in the air at once. Panel 3 draws both timelines on the same scale so the stretch is measured, not asserted. Change the modulation and only the bit count changes: 16-QAM would take 64 bits to fill the same two blocks.
  • The cyclic prefix does two jobs, and it is not free. Each block is sent with its own last sample repeated in front of it, 50 ns of it here. The first job is the one the picture shows: the echo of the previous block lands in that copy rather than in the data, so blocks do not smear into each other. The second job is the one that makes the result below exact rather than merely better: repeating the tail turns the channel's ordinary convolution into a circular one over the block, which is what makes each received subcarrier equal to its own gain times its own symbol with no leakage between subcarriers. Divide by that gain and the symbol comes back exactly. The price is time: the two blocks occupy 900 ns against the single carrier's 800 ns for the same 16 symbols, so OFDM is 12.5% slower here, not equal. Real systems pay a smaller share because they put many more subcarriers behind one prefix: WiFi spends 0.8 µs on a 4 µs symbol, which is 20%.
  • Panel 4 is the whole reason the two chains differ. The room's response across the 20 MHz band has one deep notch in it. The pink block is the single carrier: it occupies the whole band, so it meets gains from 0.10× to 1.90× at the same moment, and there is no single number the receiver can multiply by to undo that. The dots are the eight subcarriers on the same curve. Each one meets a single gain, so for each one a single number IS enough, and the receiver applies eight different ones.
  • Panel 5 is the result, and the honest statement of it is narrow. The single carrier block has symbols read as the wrong symbol; the OFDM block has none, and its symbols land exactly on their constellation points rather than merely close to them. What OFDM removed is the interference between symbols. It did not remove the notch.
  • Which is why subcarrier 5 deserves a sentence. The eight subcarriers meet gains of 1.90, 1.76, 1.35, 0.73, 0.10, 0.73, 1.35 and 1.76. Dividing by 0.10 recovers that symbol exactly here because this page has no noise in it, but it also multiplies whatever noise is present by ten, and subcarriers 4 and 6 at 0.73 are down as well. So the honest reading is that OFDM turned one unfixable problem into one bad subcarrier and two weak ones out of eight, and the next step in the lecture, a code spread across all the subcarriers, is what deals with those.
  • This block of bits is not special. Press Try another sixteen bits and watch the single carrier count move while the OFDM count stays at zero. Over 4000 random blocks through this same room the single carrier reads on average 1.86 of its 16 symbols wrongly and 74% of blocks contain at least one error, so a block with three errors is an ordinary one rather than a chosen one.
Sources. That OFDM transmits over a large number of narrow subcarriers instead of one wide carrier, and that this lengthens the symbol, is Steenkiste, Introduction to Wireless Networking and Its Impact on Applications, 2nd edition, section 3.6.1; that the resulting advantages are that selective fading affects only some subcarriers and that the longer symbol reduces inter-symbol interference is Stallings, Wireless Communications and Networks, 2nd edition, page 338. The serial to parallel split of one stream at R bits per second into N substreams at R over N is Stallings figure 11.11 on the same page. The cyclic prefix, and the rule that it must be longer than the delay spread of the channel, is Stallings page 339. The two tap room, with an echo 0.9 as strong as the direct copy, is the same model this course uses on its multipath and selective fading pages, so the curve in panel 4 is the curve the class has already met. The model on this page is exact arithmetic on complex numbers and carries no noise; the build chain recomputes every number here in Python and compares the two point by point.
