One carrier or four, through the same room

The same sixteen bits are sent twice through the same room: once as one fast stream on a single carrier, and once split across four slower subcarriers. Nothing about the room changes between the two runs. Step through and watch where the two chains part company, which is not at the transmitter and not in the air, but at the moment the receiver has to decide what each symbol was.

What to look at

  • The room is one echo, and it is the same room in both runs. A direct copy, and a second copy 0.9 as strong arriving 50 ns later, which is 15 m of extra path. That is the echo amplitude every other page in this course uses, and 15 m is an ordinary large room. Nothing else is in the model: no noise, no movement, no second room.
  • The single carrier runs at 20 Msym/s, so a symbol lasts 50 ns. The echo is therefore exactly one symbol late, and every symbol arrives on top of the one before it. That is inter-symbol interference, and panel 2 shows it as a time picture rather than as a phrase.
  • Four subcarriers, so each symbol lasts four times as long. 200 ns instead of 50 ns, carrying the same eight symbols, because four of them are in the air at once. The echo is still 50 ns, so it now covers a quarter of a symbol instead of all of it. Panel 3 draws the two timelines on the same scale so the stretch is measured, not asserted.
  • The cyclic prefix is what makes the quarter harmless rather than merely smaller, and it is not free. Each OFDM block is sent with its own last sample repeated in front of it, 50 ns of it here. The echo lands in that copy and not in the data, and the arithmetic that follows is exact rather than approximate. The price is time: the two OFDM blocks occupy 500 ns against the single carrier's 400 ns for the same eight symbols, so OFDM is 25% slower here, not equal. Count it on the two timelines in panels 2 and 3. Real systems pay a smaller share because they use many more subcarriers for the same prefix: WiFi spends 0.8 µs of prefix on a 4 µs symbol, which is 20%. Remove the prefix altogether and OFDM loses the clean result below, which is why no real system does.
  • Panel 4 is the whole reason the two chains differ. The room's response across the 20 MHz band has one deep notch in it. The single carrier occupies the whole band, so it meets the notch and everything either side of it at once, and there is no single number the receiver can multiply by to undo that. OFDM meets the same curve, but each subcarrier meets only one point of it, so for each subcarrier there IS a single number, and the receiver applies four of them.
  • Panel 5 is the result, and the honest statement of it is narrow. The single carrier block has symbols read as the wrong symbol; the OFDM block has none, and its symbols land exactly on their constellation points rather than merely close to them. What OFDM removed is the interference between symbols. It did not remove the notch.
  • Which is why subcarrier 3 deserves a sentence. The four subcarriers meet gains of 1.90, 1.35, 0.10 and 1.35. Dividing by 0.10 recovers the symbol exactly here because this page has no noise in it, but it also multiplies whatever noise is present by ten. So the honest reading is that OFDM turned one unfixable problem into one weak subcarrier out of four, and the next step in the lecture, a code spread across all the subcarriers, is what deals with the weak one.
  • This block of bits is not special. Press Try another sixteen bits and watch the single carrier count move while the OFDM count stays at zero. Over 4000 random blocks through this same room the single carrier reads on average 0.68 of its 8 symbols wrongly and 53% of blocks contain at least one error, so a block with two errors is an ordinary one rather than a chosen one.
Sources. That OFDM transmits over a large number of narrow subcarriers instead of one wide carrier, and that this lengthens the symbol, is Steenkiste, Introduction to Wireless Networking and Its Impact on Applications, 2nd edition, section 3.6.1; that the resulting advantages are that selective fading affects only some subcarriers and that the longer symbol reduces inter-symbol interference is Stallings, Wireless Communications and Networks, 2nd edition, page 338. The serial to parallel split of one stream at R bits per second into N substreams at R over N is Stallings figure 11.11 on the same page. The cyclic prefix, and the rule that it must be longer than the delay spread of the channel, is Stallings page 339. The two tap room, with an echo 0.9 as strong as the direct copy, is the same model this course uses on its multipath and selective fading pages, so the curve in panel 4 is the curve the class has already met. The model on this page is exact arithmetic on complex numbers and carries no noise; the build chain recomputes every number here in Python and compares the two point by point.
