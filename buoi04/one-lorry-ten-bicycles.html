One lorry, or ten bicycles

A road with ten lanes, and ten parcels to deliver. Send one lorry straddling every lane, or ten bicycles one per lane. A rock sits in one lane. The two convoys cover the same road in the same time, and the rock costs them very different amounts. Section 2 drops the metaphor and runs the same comparison on a real indoor channel and a real 20 MHz WiFi band.

What the picture means

The road is the channel. Across the road, the lanes are frequencies. A vehicle that straddles every lane is using the whole band at once. A vehicle in one lane is using one narrow slice. Along the road, distance is time. A short vehicle occupies the band only briefly; a long one occupies its lane for much longer.

That is why the lorry is wide and short and each bicycle is narrow and long. It is not an artistic choice: a narrow slice cannot carry quick changes, so a narrow vehicle has to be a long one. Both convoys cover exactly the same rectangle of road, which is why they set out together and arrive together. Nothing has been traded away for safety.

The rock is a notch in the channel, sitting at one frequency and staying there. The lorry meets it because the lorry is in every lane, and it loses everything it carries. The bicycles meet it in one lane only, so one parcel is lost and the rest roll past.

Where the metaphor is honest, and where it is not

  • Honest: the same work in the same time. Both convoys deliver the same number of parcels over the same stretch of road. The slow bicycle is slow because it is narrow, and there are as many of them as the lorry is wide.
  • Honest: one rock, very different damage. A single fixed obstacle removes everything from a vehicle that spans the whole band, and one parcel in ten from a convoy that does not.
  • Honest: the code finishes the job. Nine parcels that arrive can carry enough redundancy to rebuild the tenth. Nine parcels that never set out cannot.
  • Not honest: rocks do not sit still. A real notch moves when anything in the room moves, which is why a receiver measures the band again and again rather than learning the bad lanes once.
  • Not honest: bicycles do not interfere. Real subcarriers overlap heavily in frequency and avoid each other only because their spacing is exact. No lane markings are that good.
  • Not honest: a rock is sharp, a notch is not. A notch attenuates its neighbourhood gradually, so lanes near the rock are slowed rather than stopped.

Section 2: the same story without the metaphor

Above, a rock in a lane. Here, the real thing: a measured-looking indoor channel, a real 20 MHz WiFi band, and a slider that cuts the band into slices. Nothing about the room changes when you move that slider. The only thing that changes is how wide each signal is, and therefore how much of the channel's unevenness any one signal has to swallow. Reading the three panels

Panel 1 is the room. It is the gain of a four-path indoor channel plotted against frequency across one 20 MHz WiFi channel, and it does not move when you change the number of slices. The blocks drawn on it are the signals: one wide block, or many narrow ones. Teal means the slice came through, magenta means it did not.

Panel 2 is the whole argument in one picture. Each bar is how much the gain varies across that one signal, which is the quantity that decides whether the signal is treated evenly. The dashed line near the top is what a single wide carrier has to swallow. Press Switch to OFDM and watch every bar collapse to the floor: in this room the figure falls from 13.69 dB for one wide carrier to a typical 0.47 dB per slice, about twenty nine times smaller. The room did not improve. The signals got narrower.

Panel 3 is what arrives. One cell per slice. The wide carrier is a single cell, so when it fails there is nothing left. With 64 slices the failures are a minority, and a code carrying a little redundancy rebuilds them, which is the slide on coding in the lecture.

Two different ways a slice can fail, and the difference matters

This page marks a slice failed for either of two reasons, and colours the panel 2 bar accordingly, because they are not the same problem and they do not have the same cure.

  • Selective: the gain varies by more than 3 dB across the slice. The slice is treated unevenly, so its own spectrum comes out distorted and its symbols smear into each other. Power does not cure this. It is the failure that dominates when the signal is wide, and it is the one OFDM removes.
  • Weak: the slice sits in a notch, average gain below −6 dB. The slice is treated evenly but faintly. This one power or coding can cure, and OFDM does not remove it, it merely confines it to a few slices instead of ruining everything.
  • Watch the two trade places as you slide. At one slice the failure is entirely selectivity. By 32 slices no slice is selective at all, and every remaining failure is a slice that fell in a notch. That exchange is what OFDM actually does: it converts a problem that cannot be coded around into one that can.
  • More slices stops helping. Step from 64 to 128 and the typical slice gets flatter still, but the count of failures does not keep falling, because the notch is a fixed width of the band and slicing it finer just divides the same bad region into more pieces. The gain from narrowing runs out once each slice is already flat.
  • A rougher room needs more than slicing. Press A rougher room: the echoes arrive twice as late, the notches crowd twice as close, and some slices turn selective again even at 64. That is the honest limit, and it is why a real system also carries a cyclic prefix and a code rather than relying on narrow slices alone.
Sources. That a narrower subcarrier must carry a longer symbol is the reciprocal relation between bandwidth and symbol time, taught in session 2 part 3 through Nyquist.
