Overlapping without interfering

Frequency division multiplexing keeps a gap between neighbours so they cannot reach each other. OFDM removes the gaps and lets the neighbours overlap heavily, and still reads each one cleanly. This page shows how that is possible, and it shows it in the place the magic actually happens, which is not the spectrum but the receiver's integrator.

Section 1: where the two schemes put their carriers

Both schemes carry the same number of subcarriers. FDM gives each one a band-limited shape and spaces them so they just touch, which costs a guard band on every gap. OFDM spaces them exactly one subcarrier width apart, so they overlap heavily. Move the guard slider to nothing and the two become the same width, which is the honest statement of what the guard actually buys and costs.

Section 2: why the overlap does not interfere

The receiver reads one subcarrier by multiplying the arriving waveform by that subcarrier's own frequency and adding up the result over exactly one symbol. That is the whole operation, and orthogonality is a statement about what it returns. The quickest way to read the panels is to compare two of them. Panel c is that multiply done with the wanted subcarrier itself, and it is never negative: a wave times itself is a square. Panel b is the same multiply done with a neighbour, and it spends exactly as long above the line as below. Panel d turns that into the only question that matters, which is whether two bars are the same length. They are, so the neighbour weighs nothing, and the reason is one sentence: the neighbour's product completes a whole number of ups and downs inside the window, and a whole number of ups and downs weighs nothing. Nothing is filtered out. It cancels itself.

Section 3: the window is what does the work, not the spacing

A natural objection to section 2, and the right one to raise. A modulated signal is not periodic, so it is not one frequency: it occupies a band around its carrier, and the carrier is only one component of it. Send it and you send that whole set of components. So even if the component at the exact grid frequency is clean, surely the rest of the band still overlaps the neighbours and still gets contaminated.

Every clause of that is true except the conclusion, and this section shows where it parts company with OFDM. Within one OFDM symbol a subcarrier is not modulated at all. Its amplitude and phase are held constant for the whole symbol, and the modulation happens only between symbols. So over the receiver's window a subcarrier is a pure tone, described completely by one complex number, and there is no band to reassemble. The band in the objection is real, but it only exists relative to an observation longer than one symbol. Panel f below is that band, measured over four symbols, and it does overlap the neighbours exactly as the objection says.

Then drag Slip the window. Panel f does not move, because nothing about the transmitted signal changes. The constellation in panel g collapses anyway. The spacing is not what separates the subcarriers. The window is.

What section 3 settles

  • The objection is right about the spectrum. Panel f measures one subcarrier over four consecutive symbols with different data in each, which is what a long observation sees. It is not a line. It is a band that reaches well past its neighbours' centre frequencies, just as the objection says.
  • The receiver never takes that long observation. It resets every symbol. Over one symbol the same subcarrier is a constant-amplitude tone, so the composite is exactly N lines on the grid with nothing between them, which is the fact session 2 part 3 established about any signal that repeats with period T.
  • Length is not the problem; alignment is. Press Three whole symbols, still aligned. The window is three times longer, and the reading is still exact, because every subcarrier is still a constant-amplitude tone throughout it. Then slip that same window by a twentieth of a symbol and it fails. What breaks orthogonality is not how much you look at, but whether a subcarrier's amplitude changes while you are looking.
  • So the whole-number-of-cycles rule has a second half. Section 2 asked that the product complete a whole number of cycles in the window. That requires the window to be a whole number of symbol periods and to start where a symbol starts. Miss either and the condition fails.
  • Push the slider all the way and it comes good again. At a hundred per cent the window has moved by a whole symbol, so it is aligned once more, this time with the next symbol, and the reading is exact again. Near the ends it is nearly exact for the same reason. So the damage is not a function of how far you have moved but of how far you are from the nearest boundary, which is the same statement as the one above in different words.
  • This is exactly what the cyclic prefix is for. With echoes in the room, the tail of the previous symbol arrives during this one, so what sits inside the receiver's window is a signal whose amplitude jumps partway through, which is the failure this section demonstrates with the slider. The guard interval exists to give the receiver a window in which no such jump occurs.

The one sentence to take away

Two subcarriers are orthogonal over a symbol when their product completes a whole number of cycles inside that symbol, because a sine wave swept over a whole number of cycles encloses exactly as much area above the axis as below, and the two cancel. Panel 2 is that sentence drawn: the product of a carrier at a cycles and one at b cycles is a pair of waves at b−a and b+a cycles, and when a and b are both whole numbers so are their sum and difference. That is the entire content of the word orthogonal here, and it is also why the subcarrier spacing has to be exactly one over the symbol length and nothing near it.

Things worth noticing

  • The interference is present, and it is large. Press Exactly on the grid and look at the product trace in the middle panel before looking at the total. It is not small and it is not zero. At many instants during the symbol the neighbour is contributing more than the wanted carrier is. Students often picture orthogonality as the neighbours somehow being kept apart. They are not kept apart at all; their contributions arrive in full and then sum to nothing.
  • It cancels only at the end, and only at the right end. Watch the running total pass through large positive and negative values on its way. If the receiver stopped integrating early, or carried on late, it would read whatever the total happened to be at that moment, which is not zero. Orthogonality is a property of the signals and of the window the receiver uses, exactly as flat and selective were properties of the channel and the signal bandwidth together.
  • Move it off the grid and the cancellation fails immediately. A nudge of a twentieth of a spacing is already enough to leave a visible residue. This is not a tolerance band with a comfortable margin; it is an exact condition, which is why the subcarriers are computed as one block of arithmetic rather than generated by separate oscillators.
  • Distance does not help much, and that is surprising. Set the offset to a quarter and then walk the interferer from one subcarrier away to four. The leakage falls, but slowly, roughly as one over the distance. A neighbour four slices away still leaks. So OFDM does not survive a frequency error by having most subcarriers far apart; it survives only by having the error be zero.
  • One reference on its own can lie to you. The receiver correlates against both a cosine and a sine at the subcarrier frequency, and the page draws both running totals. At an offset of exactly half a spacing with a phase of zero, the cosine total lands on zero while the sine total does not. A page that drew only the cosine would appear to show orthogonality surviving in precisely the case where it fails worst. Judge by the length of the pair, which is the Leakage chip, not by either total alone.
  • The phase moves the leakage between the two totals but never removes it. Set any non-zero offset and sweep the phase slider through a full turn. The split between the cosine total and the sine total changes continuously, and the leakage chip stays away from zero the whole way round. On the grid, the same sweep keeps it at zero the whole way round.

Where this page simplifies

  • The wanted subcarrier sits at two cycles per symbol. A real 802.11a subcarrier sits at a few thousand cycles per symbol, which cannot be drawn. The argument does not depend on the number being small: it depends only on it being a whole number.
  • Only one interferer is drawn. A real symbol carries all the other subcarriers at once. Each of them is separately orthogonal to the one being read, and a sum of zeros is zero, so nothing in the argument changes.
  • The FDM shape is a choice this page makes. Real FDM filters are built to a specification rather than to a formula, so the exact shape above is illustrative. What is not illustrative is the structure of the comparison: FDM pays a gap on every boundary and OFDM pays none.
  • The channel is perfect here. This page is about the receiver's arithmetic, so there is no multipath and no noise. Multipath is what the previous pages covered, and the cyclic prefix exists precisely to keep multipath from breaking the whole-number-of-cycles condition that this page relies on.
