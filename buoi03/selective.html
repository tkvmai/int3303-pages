Flat and selective fading

One room, one channel, and one question: does the whole of your signal move together, or do parts of it get hit while other parts do not? The answer is not a property of the room alone. It depends on how wide your signal is, and you can change that here without touching anything else.

Panel 1: what you are looking at

This is the channel, drawn as gain against frequency. It is computed, not sketched: the direct path and the echo are summed as waves with their real phases, exactly as the add-or-cancel slide did, and the only difference here is that the sum is evaluated at every frequency across the band instead of at one carrier. The room is two paths throughout this page: one direct, one echo arriving ΔL later and 0.9 as strong.

The shaded block marks which frequencies your signal occupies, and nothing else. Its top and bottom edges are the edges of the frame, so its height carries no meaning. The strength of the signal, and what the channel does to that strength, is the subject of the panels below: this panel answers only where on the curve are you sitting, and how much of the curve do you span.

Why this axis is in decibels while the panels below are linear. What the channel does is a multiplication, one frequency at a time: what arrives at a frequency is what was sent at that frequency, times the channel's gain at that frequency. Panels 4 to 6 draw that multiplication, so they use a plain linear ratio. In decibels the same statement becomes an addition, because a logarithm turns a product into a sum: a component sent at 0 dB through −6 dB of channel arrives at −6 dB. Multiply in linear, add in decibels, one physical statement written two ways. This panel is in decibels because a twenty decibel notch and a six decibel ripple both have to be legible in one frame; the panels below are linear because the shape of a spectrum is what the receiver has to recover, and a logarithmic axis changes that shape.

Zero decibels on this axis is the level that would arrive with one path and no echo. Path loss has been divided out, so 0 dB is not the transmitted power: over any real distance the direct ray alone arrives tens of decibels down, and that loss is the subject of session 3 part 2 rather than of this page. With the echo at 0.9 of the direct ray the curve runs from about +5.6 dB where the two add to −20 dB where they nearly cancel.

The one experiment to run

Press Flat: a 5 MHz signal, then Selective: widen it to 160 MHz. Nothing about the room changes between those two: same two paths, same path length difference, same notches in the same places. Only the width of the signal changes. The gain across the signal goes from 0.77 dB to 25.57 dB, the verdict flips, and in the last panel the constellation goes from every symbol read correctly to three of eight read as the wrong symbol. That is the whole of Stallings' point in one slider, and it is why the term selective is defined relative to the bandwidth of the signal rather than as a property of the channel.

Things worth noticing

  • Width alone does not decide the outcome either. The textbook example: 80 MHz and Same 80 MHz, every symbol misread are the same width, and they even vary by similar amounts, 21.74 dB against 25.58 dB. Yet the first reads every symbol correctly and the second loses all eight. What differs is where the notch falls inside the band: in the first the band runs from a peak down into a notch at its edge, so most of the signal is in good gain; in the second the notch sits inside the band, so the components on either side of it are pulled apart in opposite directions. Bandwidth sets how much of the curve you span, and the variation number tells you the range, but neither tells you the shape over that span, and the shape is what breaks the symbols.
  • Narrow is relative to the notch, not an absolute. Leave the signal on the notch and widen it step by step. The verdict flips to selective well before 160 MHz, because this notch is only about 17 MHz wide at its ten decibel points. A signal is narrowband or wideband with respect to the structure of the particular channel it is sitting in, which is exactly why Stallings defines the term relative to the signal bandwidth and refuses to attach a number to it.
  • Where the notch spacing comes from. Two paths differing by ΔL put a whole extra wavelength into the longer path every time the frequency rises by c / ΔL, so the pattern repeats with that period. A long echo means a small period, which is why pushing the path length difference up crowds the notches together and makes even a narrow signal look selective. The chip above reports what fraction of one such period your signal covers.
  • The verdict needs a number, and neither textbook gives one. This page calls the channel selective when the gain varies by more than 3 dB across the signal. That three is a convention of this page and nothing more. Stallings states the test in words only: if attenuation occurs over a portion of the bandwidth of the signal, the fading is selective; nonselective fading implies the signal bandwidth is narrower than, and completely covered by, the spectrum affected by the fading. The quantity that would make the boundary numerical is the coherence bandwidth, which appears in neither book for this course and is therefore not taught in it.
  • This is why wide channels are hard. Every WiFi generation has widened the channel: 20, then 40, then 80, then 160 MHz. The bandwidth slider walks exactly those numbers. Hold the room fixed and step up through them: 3.13 dB, 6.73 dB, 21.74 dB, 25.57 dB. The capacity you are buying comes with a channel that is more selective at every step, and session 4 is about what to do with that.
  • How big a room do these path lengths describe. The slider is in metres of extra path, and it is worth converting once, because the numbers the measurement literature quotes are in nanoseconds. Light covers 300 metres in a microsecond, so an extra metre is 3.3 ns of extra delay. The default, 1.8 m, is the single bounce Steenkiste works through, and it is 6 ns. 20 m of extra path is 67 ns. Molisch reports that indoor office environments typically show delay spreads between 10 and 100 ns without line of sight, and that indoor residential rooms sit lower, around 5 to 10 ns. So the default on this page is a residential room, and an office is several times more dispersive than that. Press An office room to put the slider where the measurements are.
  • Why this matters for 20 MHz in particular. Notches repeat every c / ΔL, so at 20 m they come every 15 MHz, which is closer together than a 20 MHz channel is wide. Once that happens the band cannot avoid a notch, wherever you put the carrier: sweep the carrier slider from one end to the other and the variation stays at 25.58 dB the whole way, and at least four of the eight symbols are misread at every position. Compare that with the default room, where the same 20 MHz signal is read perfectly at most carrier positions and only occasionally falls foul of a notch. That is the real argument against one wide carrier: not that it always fails, but that whether it fails is decided by where the notches happen to land, and the transmitter does not control the room.
  • What two paths cannot show you. This page keeps the room at one direct path and one echo, so the curve is an exact cosine and the notches are evenly spaced. A real room has many echoes at many delays, and its curve is irregular: dips of different depths at uneven spacings. The periodic version is kept here because it is the one you can reason about, and because it makes the origin of the spacing visible. Nothing in the argument above depends on the regularity, only on the spacing being comparable to the signal bandwidth, which is a property of the delay spread rather than of the number of paths.

Panels 2 to 7: the same channel, now with a real signal through it

Panel 1 treated the signal as a block of spectrum. The panels below build it from the beginning, through the same channel and the same controls: bits, then symbols, then a waveform, then its spectrum, then the channel acting on each frequency component separately, then the components added back up at the receiver, and finally the constellation the receiver actually reads. Six more steps, every one of them computed rather than drawn. Change any control and panel 1 and these panels move together, because there is only one room and one signal on this page.

Why the spectrum is a set of lines, and not a curve

This is the step that surprises people, so it has its own control. A signal that repeats with period T contains only the frequencies 0, 1/T, 2/T, 3/T and so on, and nothing in between. That is the Fourier series, and it is the whole reason the spectrum here is a row of bars rather than a continuous shape. The page takes a block of N symbols and treats that block as one period, so T = N × Ts and the lines come out 1/T apart.

Drag the block length slider and watch the top of the chip row. Four symbols give lines 1.25 MHz apart; eight symbols halve that to 0.63; thirty-two symbols bring it down to 0.16 and the bars start to merge into what looks like a solid shape. Carry that to its limit: a real data stream never repeats, so T is effectively infinite, the spacing goes to zero, and the spectrum is continuous. The lines on this page are therefore a property of the window we chose to look through, not a property of the modulation. They are drawn because a component you can point at is worth more here than a component hidden inside a curve, and nothing about the argument changes when the lines merge.

So yes, a block length of eight means the model has the transmitter sending those eight symbols over and over, for ever. Two things are worth saying about that assumption, because it is the one students query.

It is not a trick peculiar to this page. Every numerical spectrum carries the same assumption. Feed a block of samples to a discrete Fourier transform and the transform treats that block as one period, whether anyone says so or not. "The block repeats" and "I took the transform of this block" are the same sentence. That is also why a spectrum analyser shows you bins rather than a true curve.

Where it differs from reality is at the two edges. In a real stream the symbol before the first one is some other symbol, not the last one in the block, and the tail of the echo that runs off the end of the block would spill into the next block of data rather than wrapping round to the start. With eight symbols at 5 Msym/s the 6 ns echo is 0.4 percent of the block and the difference is invisible; at 80 Msym/s it is 6 percent, and what difference there is sits on the first symbol. None of it touches the lesson: the channel multiplies each frequency by its own gain whether the spectrum is a row of lines or a continuous curve.

What each panel shows

  • 2, the bits. The raw stream, grouped into symbols. With 16-QAM each group of four bits becomes one symbol: the first two bits choose the horizontal level, the last two the vertical one, using the Gray mapping from session 2 part 2.
  • 3, the symbols as a waveform. Each symbol becomes a pulse of its own height, and the pulses are added, then the sum is carried on the sine wave, so what is drawn is the real transmitted waveform and not one of its two branches. Notice the pulses overlap in time, which is deliberate and not a fault: a raised cosine pulse is shaped so that at each symbol instant, marked by the ticks, every other pulse is passing through zero.
  • 4, the spectrum of that waveform. One bar per frequency component, spacing 1/T as explained above. This is the same waveform, written differently.
  • 5, the channel, component by component. The channel gain is drawn across the signal's own stretch of spectrum, and each bar is multiplied by the value of that curve at its own frequency. Grey is what was sent, colour is what arrived.
  • 6, adding the components back up. The received waveform is nothing more than the sum of the received bars. Drag the rebuild slider from 1 upwards and watch it assemble: a few components give a crude shape, all of them give the exact received signal. This is where the flat and selective cases part company. In the flat case every component was scaled by the same factor, so the sum is the transmitted waveform at a different size. In the selective case the components were scaled differently, so the sum is a different waveform, and no amount of volume will turn it back into the original.
  • 7, the symbols the receiver reads. Sampled at the symbol instants, after the receiver has chosen its sampling moment and corrected one overall complex gain. Open circles are the alphabet, dotted lines are the decision boundaries, and each arrow runs from where a symbol was sent to where it arrived. An arrow that crosses a boundary is a symbol the receiver reads as a different symbol, and those are drawn in red and ringed.

The number that decides whether it matters

The last chip divides the largest symbol error by half the distance to the nearest constellation point, which is exactly how far a symbol may stray before it is read as its neighbour, and it reports the worst symbol rather than the average, because one symbol over the line is already a wrong symbol. Above 1.00 means at least one symbol was misread.
