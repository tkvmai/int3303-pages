Flat and selective fading

One room, one channel, and one question: does the whole of your signal move together, or do parts of it get hit while other parts do not? The answer is not a property of the room alone. It depends on how wide your signal is, and you can change that here without touching anything else.

What you are looking at

The upper plot is the channel, drawn as gain against frequency. It is computed, not sketched: every path is summed as a wave with its real phase, exactly as the add-or-cancel slide did, and the only difference here is that the sum is evaluated at every frequency across the band instead of at one carrier. The shaded block is the stretch of spectrum your signal occupies.

The lower plot is what comes out: the transmitted spectrum, drawn flat and dashed, multiplied by the channel. That product is the thing the receiver has to demodulate, and when it is no longer the shape that was sent, the signal has been distorted rather than merely weakened.

The one experiment to run

Press The textbook example, then Same room, a 5 MHz radio. Nothing about the room changed between those two: same paths, same path length difference, same notches in the same places. Only the width of the signal changed, and the verdict flipped. That is the whole of Stallings' point, and it is why the term selective is defined relative to the bandwidth of the signal rather than as a property of the channel.

Things worth noticing

  • Flat does not mean harmless. Press A 1 MHz signal parked in a notch. The verdict is flat, because the whole of that narrow signal moves together: the gain varies by only about 0.4 dB across it. But the mean gain is nearly twenty decibels down and the link is in serious trouble. Then press parked on a peak: same width, same room, and the signal is now about 5.6 dB stronger than it would have been with no multipath at all. Flat and selective answer the question is the band treated unevenly, not the question is the signal any good.
  • Narrow is relative to the notch, not an absolute. Leave the signal on the notch and widen it from 1 MHz to 5 MHz. The verdict flips to selective, because this notch is only about 17 MHz wide at its ten decibel points, so a 5 MHz signal already straddles its walls. A signal is narrowband or wideband with respect to the structure of the particular channel it is sitting in, which is exactly why Stallings defines the term relative to the signal bandwidth and refuses to attach a number to it.
  • Where the notch spacing comes from. Two paths differing by ΔL put a whole extra wavelength into the longer path every time the frequency rises by c / ΔL, so the pattern repeats with that period. A long echo means a small period, which is why pushing the path length difference up crowds the notches together and makes even a narrow signal look selective.
  • More paths, less regularity. With two paths the channel is a clean repeating shape. Push the number of paths up and it becomes ragged: the notches are no longer evenly spaced and no longer the same depth. A real indoor channel looks like the ragged one. The two-path case is kept as the default only because its period can be written down.
  • The verdict needs a number, and neither textbook gives one. This page calls the channel selective when the gain varies by more than 3 dB across the signal. That three is a convention of this page and nothing more. Stallings states the test in words only: if attenuation occurs over a portion of the bandwidth of the signal, the fading is selective; nonselective fading implies the signal bandwidth is narrower than, and completely covered by, the spectrum affected by the fading. The quantity that would make the boundary numerical is the coherence bandwidth, which appears in neither book for this course and is therefore not taught in it.
  • This is why wide channels are hard. Every WiFi generation has widened the channel: 20, then 40, then 80, then 160 MHz. Hold the room fixed on this page and walk the bandwidth up through those numbers. The capacity you are buying comes with a channel that is more selective at every step, and session 4 is about what to do with that.
