Where the channel response comes from

A channel response is not something anyone draws. It is something you measure. Send one test tone, see how big the received sum is, write down one dot. Change the tone, write down another dot. Do it across the whole band and the dots are the curve. This page lets you do exactly that, one tone at a time or as a sweep.

What to do first

Press A tone where the two copies add, then A tone where the two copies cancel. Look only at the top two rows. The echo is late by the same 5 ns in both cases, and the orange bracket showing that lateness is literally the same width on the screen. What changes is the tone: at 200 MHz one cycle also lasts 5 ns, so the echo comes back exactly one cycle behind and lands on top of the original. At 500 MHz one cycle lasts 2 ns, so 5 ns is two and a half cycles, and the echo comes back upside down.

That is the entire mechanism. A fixed delay is a different fraction of a cycle at every frequency, so the same two paths add at some frequencies and cancel at others.

Then build the curve

Drag the test tone slider slowly, or press Sweep. Each tone you visit leaves one dot on the bottom plot, at the height of the sum you just measured. Nothing is being drawn for you: the shape appears because it is the record of the measurements. That shape is the channel frequency response.

Why it needs multipath

Set the number of paths to 1 and sweep again. The curve is a flat line. With one path there is no second copy, so there is nothing whose phase could depend on frequency, and every tone comes out the same size. The ripple is not a property of the room, of the distance, or of the equipment. It is a property of having more than one copy arrive. Put the paths back to 2 and it returns.

A word about the extra paths

The first echo is the one you control: its extra length is the slider, and at the default of 1.5 m it arrives 5 ns late. The second and third echoes, which appear when you raise the number of paths, sit at fixed multiples of that: 1.68 and 2.80 times the slider, so at the default they arrive 8.40 ns and 14.00 ns late, and each is weaker than the one before it. Those multipliers are an illustrative set, not measurements. They were chosen to be unevenly spaced, so that the channel comes out ragged rather than neatly periodic, and no textbook or measurement campaign is behind the particular values. What is real here is the mechanism, not the room. A real room would have its own lengths, and many more of them.

Things worth trying

  • Change how long the extra path is. The delay changes, and so does the spacing of the peaks on the curve. Two copies add whenever the delay is a whole number of cycles, which happens every time the frequency rises by one divided by the delay. A longer extra path means a longer delay, which means the peaks crowd together.
  • Weaken the echo. At strength 0.9 the two copies nearly annihilate each other and the notches go very deep. At strength 0.3 the notches are shallow, because a weak echo can only remove a little of the direct copy. The peaks and notches sit in exactly the same places either way: the strength of the echo sets how deep the notches are, the length of the path sets where they are.
  • Four paths. The curve stops being a clean repeating shape and becomes ragged, with notches of different depths at uneven spacings. A real indoor channel looks like this. The two-path case is worth keeping as the mental model only because its spacing can be written down in one line.
  • Watch the top two rows during a sweep. The sum grows and shrinks as the tone moves, and the bottom plot is just a record of how big it got. Students often think the response curve is a different kind of object from the waveform. It is not: it is a summary of one number taken from the waveform, at every frequency.
A note on the frequencies. This page works at a few hundred megahertz so that you can see the cycles and the delay in the same picture, and it starts from an extra path of 1.5 m because that gives a delay of exactly 5 ns and therefore peaks at round multiples of 200 MHz. At 5 GHz, where the later lecture figures are drawn, a 1.8 m extra path is 6 ns of delay, but 6 ns there is 30 cycles rather than 1, which cannot be drawn on a screen. Nothing else differs, and the one thing worth carrying across is the rule rather than the particular numbers: the spacing of the peaks is one divided by the delay, wherever in the spectrum you look, because it depends on the path length difference and on nothing else. The sources are Steenkiste, Introduction to Wireless Networking and Its Impact on Applications, 2nd edition, section 2.6.2, and Stallings, Wireless Communications and Networks, 2nd edition, section 5.4. The model on this page is the one used by the lecture figures, and the build chain compares the two point by point.
