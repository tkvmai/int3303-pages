A late symbol landing on the next one

Each symbol is a stretch of carrier held at one amplitude and one phase. One signal leaves the transmitter, two copies arrive, and the receiver only ever has their sum. Because the second copy is late, part of one symbol is still arriving while the next symbol is being received. The time window stays the same length whatever you do, so the delay keeps one width on screen and only the symbol boundaries move.

The receiver never compares waveforms. It multiplies the slot by the carrier twice, once against a cosine and once against a sine, averages each product over one whole symbol, and is left with two numbers. That pair is the point. Multipath changes the waveform a great deal and still moves the pair only a little, which is why the symbol usually survives: the decision asks only which of the 16 points the pair is nearest to, and there is room to be wrong by almost half the distance to the neighbour before the answer changes.

What the receiver ends up with

Each symbol is one point of the 16-QAM constellation from session 2 part 2. The receiver measures the amplitude and the phase of whatever is in the slot, so when a piece of the previous symbol is still there, it measures the wrong point. Pale grey marks the 16 points the transmitter is allowed to send, and the faint grid is the decision boundary.

The three arrows are the three symbols of the drawing above: dark is where each one was sent, pink is where it was received. Symbol 1 has nothing before it, so it arrives undamaged and its arrow has no length. Symbols 2 and 3 are each pushed away from their own point by the symbol that came before.

Why this cannot simply be subtracted out. How far symbol 2 is pushed depends on which symbol happened to precede it, and that is data, not a property of the channel. Tick the box under the picture to see every possibility at once. There are sixteen of them because 16-QAM has sixteen symbols and any one of them could have been the previous one. The receiver has no way of knowing which arrow it is on, and that is why a late copy behaves like noise.

What decides whether the symbol is misread

Only one thing: whether the point is pushed more than half the distance to the neighbouring point. Everything else feeds one of those two sides.

  • The push is g × (the previous symbol's coordinate), where g = a·β / (1 + a(1 − β)) is what is left of the previous symbol after the receiver has corrected the current one's own gain. So echo strength and spill fraction both feed it, and the readout prints g directly.
  • Which symbol came before feeds it too, and that is data, not channel. Tick the box under the constellation: at 16-QAM a corner symbol pushes three times as hard as one next to the origin, and at 64-QAM seven times. The receiver cannot know which case it is in.
  • The other side, the distance to the neighbour, is set by the modulation. Packing more bits into a symbol packs the points closer together, with nothing else changed.
  • Putting the two together gives one clean threshold. With L amplitude levels on each axis, a symbol is misread once g > 1/(L − 1): 1 for QPSK, 1/3 for 16-QAM, 1/7 for 64-QAM. Same room, same echo, and 64-QAM gives way seven times sooner than QPSK. That is the price of the extra bits promised in session 2 part 2.

Two things the drawing simplifies, said out loud

  • The carrier is drawn at 10 MHz so that individual cycles are visible. A real 2.4 GHz carrier fits roughly 1200 cycles into a 500 ns symbol, which cannot be drawn.
  • Both the delay and the symbol length move in steps of 100 ns, which is exactly one cycle of that drawn carrier. So the echo always arrives in phase with the direct path, and a symbol always holds a whole number of cycles. Whether two copies add or cancel is the question of the previous slide, about path differences of λ/2; this page is about the much longer delays that make one symbol reach into the next.
  • The receiver is assumed to have corrected for the gain of the current symbol's own two copies. What is left on the constellation is interference from the previous symbol and nothing else.
  • The two low-pass filters of the textbook's demodulator are drawn here as an average over one whole symbol, which is the same idea stated as one step: both remove the double-frequency term and leave one value per symbol.

Four things worth trying

  • Set the delay to zero. The echo lands entirely inside its own slot, nothing is damaged, and the gauge stays empty however strong the echo is. A strong echo is not automatically a harmful one.
  • Hold the delay at 200 ns and shorten the symbols from 800 ns to 400 ns, which is the pair of buttons matching the two slides. The environment has not changed and the echo has not changed, yet the gauge fills from a quarter of the margin to three fifths of it. Worth saying aloud that the symbol still survives: the margin was built for exactly this.
  • Now set the delay to 400 ns and do the same thing again, 800 ns down to 500 ns, which is the next pair of buttons. This time the gauge passes its limit and the receiver reads a different symbol, with nothing changed but the symbol length. That is the point at which the room, not the bandwidth, is what limits the symbol rate.
  • Leave the channel exactly as it is and change only the modulation. QPSK survives anything this model can produce; 64-QAM fails while 16-QAM is still comfortable. Nothing about the radio or the room changed, only how closely the points were packed.
